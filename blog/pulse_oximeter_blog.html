🫁 Oxygen Delivery: The Physics Behind Every Breath

What is the point of every breath we take? What is actually happening to the oxygen we inspire? We all know the basics — oxygen enters the lungs, diffuses across the alveolar membrane into the bloodstream, binds to haemoglobin, and is carried all the way to the tissues that need it. But let's add some physics and mathematics to it — and complete the picture.

Oxygen delivery to the tissues — or DO₂ — depends on two things: how much blood the heart is pumping (cardiac output) and how much oxygen that blood is actually carrying (arterial oxygen content, CaO₂). The amount of oxygen carried by the arteries is not a random fluke value. It is a precise, calculated quantity — and it depends on very specific factors.

The CaO₂ Equation

⚡ The Equation That Ties It All Together:

CaO₂ = (Hb × SaO₂ × 1.34) + (PaO₂ × 0.0225)

  • Hüfner's constant 1.34 — every gram of haemoglobin, when fully saturated, can carry 1.34 ml of oxygen
  • The second term — accounts for the small amount of oxygen dissolved in plasma; modest in health, but never truly negligible
  • Note: You may see 1.36 or 1.39 in different textbooks — the variation exists due to different experimental conditions, but 1.34 is the most widely accepted clinical value

Types of Haemoglobin

Not all haemoglobin in our blood is the same. We know several types:

  • Oxyhaemoglobin — HbO₂ — carrying oxygen
  • Deoxyhaemoglobin — Hb — having released it
  • Carboxyhaemoglobin — COHb — bound to carbon monoxide
  • Methaemoglobin — MetHb — oxidised and unable to carry oxygen
  • Sulphaemoglobin — SulfHb — rare, but irreversible

Of these, COHb, MetHb, and SulfHb together constitute only about 1–3% in a normal healthy individual. But their presence, even in small amounts, becomes clinically significant — as we will see later.

Functional vs Fractional SaO₂ — A Critical Distinction

Functional SaO₂ — the percentage of haemoglobin that is capable of carrying oxygen that is actually carrying it. It only considers oxyhaemoglobin and deoxyhaemoglobin in its denominator.

Fractional SaO₂ — takes all forms of haemoglobin into account — functional and dysfunctional alike.

⚠️ In clinical practice, what your pulse oximeter displays is the Functional SaO₂ — and that distinction is one of its most important limitations.
🎯 EXAM PEARL: The CaO₂ equation appears deceptively simple — but it is the foundation of understanding why a patient with severe anaemia can desaturate faster, why carbon monoxide poisoning is so dangerous, and why SpO₂ can look normal even when oxygen delivery is critically impaired.

📈 The Oxygen Dissociation Curve: Nature's Safety Net

SaO₂ is not a fixed number. It is a function of PaO₂ — and that relationship is beautifully described by one of the most important graphs in all of physiology: the Oxygen Dissociation Curve (ODC).

The Shape — and Why It's Sigmoid

The curve is not a straight line. It is S-shaped — sigmoid — and that shape is not an accident. It is a consequence of cooperative binding. As each oxygen molecule binds to haemoglobin, it changes the shape of the molecule — making it easier for the next oxygen to bind. This is haemoglobin being efficient by design.

The Three Zones That Matter Clinically

Zone PaO₂ Range What's Happening
Flat upper portion 70–100 mmHg Small drop in PaO₂, minimal drop in SaO₂ — the safety margin
The shoulder ⚠️ ~60 mmHg SpO₂ ~90% — the critical inflection point
Steep lower portion 🚨 Below 60 mmHg Small drop in PaO₂, massive drop in SaO₂ — danger zone
⚡ THE CLINICAL PUNCHLINE:

The flat upper portion is nature's safety net — even if PaO₂ drops from 100 to 70, SaO₂ barely moves. But once you fall off that shoulder — below a PaO₂ of 60 mmHg and SpO₂ of 90% — you are on the steep part, and things deteriorate rapidly.

This is why SpO₂ of 90% is the alarm threshold. Not 95%. Not 85%. 90%.

Shifts — Right and Left

Shift Causes Clinical Effect
Right Shift ➡️ ↑ Temperature, ↑ CO₂, ↑ 2,3-DPG, acidosis Offloads O₂ to tissues more readily
Left Shift ⬅️ ↓ Temperature, ↓ CO₂, alkalosis, fetal Hb, COHb Holds O₂ tighter — less offloading to tissues
🎯 EXAM PEARL: Carbon monoxide causes a LEFT shift — haemoglobin holds onto oxygen more tightly. Combined with the fact that COHb doesn't carry oxygen at all, CO poisoning causes a double hit: less O₂ capacity AND impaired O₂ offloading. This is why SpO₂ can look falsely normal!

💡 From ABG to SpO₂: Why We Needed a Better Way

So we've established that PaO₂ is critically important. It sits on the x-axis of the ODC and determines where your patient is on that curve. But how do we actually measure it?

The answer is — an arterial blood gas. A needle into the radial artery, a sample of arterial blood, run through a blood gas analyser. It gives you PaO₂, pH, PaCO₂, bicarbonate — the complete picture.

But here's the problem:

  • It is invasive
  • It is a point-in-time measurement
  • It hurts
  • It requires a sample, a machine, and time
  • You cannot do it continuously on every patient, every minute, through an entire surgery or a night in the ICU
🔥 The Game-Changer Question: What if you could estimate where the patient sits on the oxygen dissociation curve — continuously, non-invasively, in real time — just by shining a light through a finger?

That is exactly what the pulse oximeter does.

SpO₂ is NOT SaO₂ — Be Precise

The pulse oximeter does NOT measure PaO₂. It measures SpO₂ — the peripheral oxygen saturation. And SpO₂ is not the same as SaO₂, though in a healthy individual with normal haemoglobin they are close enough to be clinically useful.

The GPS Analogy:

🗺️ The ODC = the map
📍 SpO₂ = your GPS location on that map
🌍 PaO₂ = the actual ground beneath your feet

The GPS is useful, reliable, and continuous — but it is not the ground itself. For the actual PaO₂, you still need an ABG.
🎯 EXAM PEARL: SpO₂ of 95% → estimate PaO₂ ≈ 80 mmHg (safe). SpO₂ of 90% → PaO₂ ≈ 60 mmHg (the shoulder — act now). This estimation is only reliable when the patient has normal haemoglobin, the ODC hasn't shifted significantly, and the oximeter is reading accurately.

🔬 The Beer-Lambert Law: How Light Reads Your Blood

The Glass of Water Analogy

Imagine you have two glasses of liquid in front of you — one red coloured and one blue coloured. Now imagine shining a beam of light through each of them. The red glass absorbs certain wavelengths of light and allows others to pass through. The blue glass does the same — but differently. Each colour absorbs light in its own unique, predictable way.

Haemoglobin behaves exactly the same way:

The Two Wavelengths That Changed Medicine:
  • OxyHaemoglobin (HbO₂) — absorbs infrared light (940 nm) more readily; allows red light (660 nm) to pass through
  • DeoxyHaemoglobin (Hb) — absorbs red light (660 nm) more readily; allows infrared light (940 nm) to pass through

The pulse oximeter shines both wavelengths through your finger and measures how much of each has passed through. The ratio of red to infrared absorption is then calculated — and from that ratio, SpO₂ is derived.

Beer-Lambert Law — The Formal Principle

Beer-Lambert Law states:
The absorption of light by a substance is directly proportional to its concentration and the path length through which the light travels.

A = ε × c × l

A = Absorbance
ε = Molar absorptivity (a constant unique to each substance)
c = Concentration of the substance
l = Path length of light

In simpler terms — the more oxyhaemoglobin present, the more infrared light is absorbed and the more red light passes through. The oximeter reads this ratio continuously, thousands of times per second, and converts it into the SpO₂ value you see on the monitor.

🎯 EXAM PEARL: Beer-Lambert Law is the theoretical foundation of pulse oximetry. But remember — in practice, the SpO₂ reading is derived from an empirically calibrated curve built from studies on healthy volunteers. It is not a direct mathematical computation. This is why accuracy degrades below SpO₂ of 70% — the volunteers could not be safely taken lower.

⚡ The AC/DC Signal: Isolating Arterial Blood

Your finger is not just haemoglobin. It has skin, bone, muscle, capillaries, venous blood, arterial blood — all of which absorb light to some degree. So how does the oximeter know it is reading arterial blood specifically?

The answer lies in the nature of the signal itself. The light absorbed by the finger has two distinct components:

⚡ The AC Component — Alternating Current (Pulsatile)

With every heartbeat, arterial blood surges through the vessels, changing the volume of blood in the path of the light — and therefore changing the amount of light absorbed. This rhythmic, pulsatile variation in absorption is the AC signal. And because it is purely arterial in origin, it represents SaO₂.

⚡ The DC Component — Direct Current (Constant)

This is the constant, non-pulsatile component. It represents light absorbed by everything that does not pulse — venous blood, capillaries, muscle, skin, bone and other tissues. This is background noise.

The R Value — The Heart of the Calculation

The oximeter continuously measures both components and calculates the ratio of AC to DC absorption at both wavelengths. This ratio, known as the R value, is then used to derive SpO₂:

R = (AC660/DC660) ÷ (AC940/DC940)

This R value is matched against an empirically derived calibration curve — built from studies on healthy volunteers — and SpO₂ is read out.

R = 1 → SpO₂ ≈ 85%
R < 1 → Higher saturations
R > 1 → Lower saturations
💡 CLINICAL INSIGHT: Every manufacturer builds their own calibration curve — which is why SpO₂ readings can vary slightly between different monitors and brands. A reading of 96% on one brand may display as 97% on another. Always use the same brand consistently for trend monitoring.

⚙️ The Functional Cycle: What Happens Every Second

The pulse oximeter runs on a precise, repeating functional cycle — and it is more intelligent than it appears. Here is what happens in each cycle:

Step 1: Red LED at 660 nm turns ON → photodetector measures transmitted light

Step 2: Red LED turns OFF; infrared LED at 940 nm turns ON → photodetector measures again

Step 3: Both LEDs turn OFF → photodetector measures ambient light in the environment (room lights, theatre lights, monitors)
🔥 The Clever Part — Ambient Light Subtraction:

By measuring ambient light separately — in that brief moment of darkness — the oximeter can subtract it from both readings. What remains is purely the signal from the two LEDs, clean and uncontaminated.

This three-step cycle repeats hundreds of times per second — faster than any physiological change can occur — giving you a continuous, real-time reading.

Once the clean signals are obtained, the oximeter calculates the R value, matches it against the calibration curve, and the final SpO₂ number appears on your monitor.

🎯 EXAM PEARL: The final SpO₂ value on your monitor is NOT a direct physical measurement. It is the output of an entire cycle: light emission → ambient subtraction → R value calculation → calibration curve matching. All of this happening silently, every fraction of a second. This is exactly why it earns its place as the first monitor on and the last monitor off.

🔧 Types of Pulse Oximeters

The pulse oximeter exists in two fundamental designs — each built around the same two core components: an emitter carrying two LED light sources at 660 nm and 940 nm, and a photodetector that measures transmitted or reflected light.

1. Transmittance Type

The emitter and detector sit on opposite sides of the tissue — most commonly across a finger, toe, or earlobe. Light passes entirely through the tissue to reach the detector.

✅ Most common type in clinical practice

2. Reflectance Type

The emitter and detector sit side by side on the same surface. Instead of passing through tissue, light is reflected back from underlying structures to the detector.

Placement options: forehead, oesophagus, chest wall — useful when peripheral probes are not feasible
✅ Used in low perfusion states where peripheral signal is unreliable
💡 CLINICAL TIP: In states of hypoperfusion, hypotension, or hypothermia — catecholamine release causes peripheral vasoconstriction and the finger probe loses signal. Move the probe to the ear or forehead. These sites are supplied by vessels less affected by catecholamine-induced vasoconstriction (ear: superficial temporal and posterior auricular arteries; forehead: supraorbital and supratrochlear branches).

⚠️ Limitations: When the Oximeter Lies

Anything that carries this many advantages — this much elegance, this much engineering — must surely come without flaws. At least, that was the thought. But as we know in medicine — every strength has a corresponding weakness. The pulse oximeter, for all its brilliance, is no exception.

Let us be precise — these are not disadvantages. They are limitations. The pulse oximeter does exactly what it is designed to do, and it does it brilliantly. But like every monitor in medicine, it has a defined scope.

What the Pulse Oximeter Does NOT Tell You

  • Tissue oxygenation — whether oxygen is actually being utilised at the cellular level
  • Ventilation status — tells you nothing about CO₂ retention or hypoventilation
  • Acid-base balance — no pH, no bicarbonate
  • PaCO₂ levels — a patient can be silently hypercapnic with a perfectly normal SpO₂
  • PaO₂ at high saturations — once SpO₂ hits 100%, the oximeter is blind. A PaO₂ of 100 mmHg and a PaO₂ of 500 mmHg both read as 100%

🚨 Hyperoxia Warning — Especially in Neonates

A SpO₂ of 100% is NOT reassurance enough. In neonates and premature infants, hyperoxia carries the risk of retinopathy of prematurity. The pulse oximeter is completely blind to the difference between PaO₂ of 100 and PaO₂ of 500.

For precise PaO₂ values at high saturations — you still need an ABG.

At the Extremes of Saturation

At higher saturations (SpO₂ > 97%): Cannot detect hyperoxia. ODC is flat here — oximeter reads 100% without distinguishing safe from dangerously high PaO₂.

At lower saturations (SpO₂ < 70%): Calibration curve becomes unreliable. The volunteer studies used to build the algorithm could not ethically go lower. Below this threshold, the SpO₂ reading is an extrapolation, not a measurement.

False Readings — The Complete Comparison Table

Cause Error Direction Mechanism
Carboxyhaemoglobin (COHb) ⬆️ Falsely HIGH COHb absorbs red light similarly to OxyHb — oximeter cannot distinguish them
Methaemoglobin (MetHb) ↔️ Pulls toward 85% MetHb absorbs both wavelengths equally — R value → 1, SpO₂ reads ~85% regardless of true saturation
Sulphaemoglobin (SulfHb) ⬇️ Falsely LOW Absorbs light differently; underestimates true saturation
Severe anaemia ⬆️ Falsely HIGH Low Hb but what remains may be well saturated — does not reflect oxygen delivery
Darker skin tone ⬆️ Falsely HIGH Melanin absorbs red light, interfering with ratio calculation — overestimates SpO₂
Nail polish / acrylic nails ⬇️ Falsely LOW Absorbs red light; artificially increases AC₆₆₀ signal
Motion artefact ↔️ Unpredictable Creates false pulsatile signal — can read high or low
Ambient light ⬇️ Falsely LOW Overwhelms photodetector despite ambient subtraction cycle
Hypoperfusion ❌ Absent / inaccurate Loss of pulsatile AC signal — no reading or unreliable
Venous pulsation ⬇️ Falsely LOW Venous congestion creates pulsatile venous signal mistaken for arterial
IV dyes (methylene blue, indocyanine green) ⬇️ Falsely LOW Absorb red light; transiently drop SpO₂ reading
🎯 EXAM PEARL — The Most Tested:
CO poisoning → Falsely HIGH SpO₂ (COHb reads like OxyHb at 660 nm)
MetHb → SpO₂ reads ~85% regardless of true saturation (R value → 1)
Methylene blue → Falsely LOW SpO₂ (absorbs red light like deoxyHb)

🕰️ History: A Century-Long Relay Race

The story of the pulse oximeter is not one of a single eureka moment. It is a relay race — a century long, passed between brilliant minds across continents.

Year Milestone Pioneer
1864 Discovered that haemoglobin is the oxygen carrier in blood — the biological truth upon which everything that followed was built Georg Gabriel Stokes
1935 Built a device that could detect oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin using light — earning him the title Father of Oximetry Karl Matthes
1940s Developed the first portable earpiece oximeter. Coined the word "oximeter" itself Glenn Millikan
1974 While working on dye dilution cardiac output studies, noticed pulsatile changes in light absorption could calculate SpO₂ non-invasively — an accidental discovery that became the foundation of modern pulse oximetry Takuo Aoyagi
1977 First fingertip pulse oximeter launched commercially Minolta
1987 Pulse oximetry became standard of care for general anaesthesia in the United States ASA Guidelines
1995 Reached everyday consumers Commercial market
2000s Signal Extraction Technology (SET) solved motion artefact; Rainbow Technology added non-invasive measurement of COHb and MetHb Masimo
2020 Frontline tool in the COVID-19 pandemic — detecting silent hypoxia in patients before symptoms appeared Global adoption

The Modern Era

No longer limited to two wavelengths, modern pulse oximeters now use multiple wavelengths of light to non-invasively measure carboxyhaemoglobin, methaemoglobin, and even total haemoglobin — the very dysfunctional species that once made the oximeter blind.

🔥 Near Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS):

Took it a step further — allowing us to peer into cerebral oxygenation during cardiac surgery, carotid endarterectomy, and neonatal intensive care. And then the pulse oximeter did something no one in Aoyagi's laboratory could have imagined — it left the hospital entirely. It found its way into smartwatches, fitness bands, and home monitoring devices.

What once filled an entire room now clips onto a finger in seconds.

🏥 Clinical Applications Across Medicine

From the operating room to the living room. From Karl Matthes shining light through an ear in 1935 to a smartwatch on a billion wrists in 2025 — the pulse oximeter has travelled further than perhaps any other monitoring device in the history of medicine.

Setting Application Special Considerations
🏥 Operating Theatre First monitor on, last monitor off ASA standard of care since 1987
🫁 ICU Continuous overnight monitoring, ventilator titration Trend monitoring more valuable than single values
🚨 Emergency Department Rapid triage, resuscitation guidance Low perfusion states — use ear or forehead probe
👶 Neonatal Unit Continuous SpO₂ monitoring Hyperoxia as dangerous as hypoxia — tight target ranges essential

⌚ Wearables Smartwatches, fitness bands Reflectance type; motion artefact a significant challenge

🎓 Exam Points: Your Victory Checklist

✅ Point #1: SpO₂ ≠ SaO₂ ≠ PaO₂
Three different values. Know the hierarchy. SpO₂ is peripheral, functional, and estimated. SaO₂ is measured at the ABG level. PaO₂ is the dissolved partial pressure.
✅ Point #2: Beer-Lambert Law = Foundation
Two wavelengths. Red 660 nm (absorbed by DeoxyHb). Infrared 940 nm (absorbed by OxyHb). R value = AC/DC ratio at both wavelengths.
✅ Point #3: CO Poisoning → Falsely HIGH SpO₂
COHb absorbs red light at 660 nm just like OxyHb. The oximeter cannot distinguish them → reads as if haemoglobin is oxygenated when it is actually poisoned.
✅ Point #4: MetHb → SpO₂ reads ~85%
MetHb absorbs equally at both wavelengths → R value → 1 → SpO₂ reads ~85% regardless of true saturation. Can be high (true SpO₂ < 85%) or low (true SpO₂ > 85%).
✅ Point #5: Functional vs Fractional SaO₂
The oximeter displays functional SpO₂ — only considers OxyHb and DeoxyHb in denominator. Ignores COHb, MetHb, SulfHb. This is why CO poisoning looks normal on the monitor.
✅ Point #6: Calibration is Empirical
Built from volunteer studies. Reliable range: 70–100%. Below 70% is extrapolation, not measurement. This is an ethical limitation — volunteers could not be safely desaturated further.
✅ Point #7: The AC Signal = Arterial Blood
Pulsatile component = arterial. Non-pulsatile (DC) = venous blood, tissue, bone. The oximeter uses the ratio of AC to DC at both wavelengths to isolate the arterial signal.
✅ Point #8: Hypoperfusion → Lose the Signal
No pulsatile signal = no SpO₂. Move to ear or forehead probe. These sites are less affected by catecholamine-induced vasoconstriction.
✅ Point #9: SpO₂ 90% = PaO₂ ~60 mmHg = Critical Shoulder
This is the inflection point of the ODC. Below this, small drops in PaO₂ cause massive drops in saturation. Act before you reach here.
✅ Point #10: The Oximeter Cannot Detect Hyperoxia
SpO₂ 100% could mean PaO₂ 100 or PaO₂ 500 — the flat upper portion of the ODC makes them indistinguishable. Critical in neonates (retinopathy risk). Always ABG when high PaO₂ matters.
