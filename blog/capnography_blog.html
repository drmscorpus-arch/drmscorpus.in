Capnography and capnometry — and why infrared beat Raman.

The monitor says SpO₂ 99%.
The heart rate is steady, the patient looks pink, and everything seems fine. But a pulse oximeter tells you about oxygenation, not ventilation. A patient breathing supplemental oxygen can hold a normal SpO₂ while CO₂ quietly climbs.

Capnography was built to fill that gap. This post covers what it measures, how to read the trace, what it is used for, where it misleads, and why infrared absorption beat Raman scattering.

§ 02

Capnometry or Capnography?

A number, or a number and a picture

Capnometry is the measurement of CO₂ in the airway gas, shown as a number: the end-tidal CO₂ (ETCO₂). Capnography is the continuous graph of CO₂ against time, called the capnogram. A capnometer gives you a number. A capnograph gives you a number and a picture, and most of the information is in the picture.

§ 03

Where the CO₂ Comes From

Production, perfusion and ventilation

CO₂ is made by cells, carried in the blood (mostly as bicarbonate), delivered to the lungs by the heart, and removed by ventilation. ETCO₂ therefore depends on three things at once: production, perfusion and ventilation. A change in any one moves the number.

ChangeETCO₂Examples
Production upRisesFever, shivering, sepsis, malignant hyperthermia
Production downFallsHypothermia, deep anaesthesia
Perfusion downFallsLow cardiac output, pulmonary embolism, cardiac arrest
Ventilation downRisesHypoventilation, rebreathing, airway obstruction
Ventilation upFallsHyperventilation

Each breath draws one waveform, and each part has a meaning.

IIIAlveolar plateauAlveolar gas. The ETCO₂ is the highest point at the end of this phase

Fig 1 — The normal capnogram: phases and angles

Two angles worth a glance

The α angle (between phases II and III) widens when expiration is obstructed. The β angle (between the plateau and the inspiratory downstroke) widens with rebreathing.

§ 06

What It Is Used For

From intubation to CPR

  • Confirming tracheal intubation. A persistent waveform over several breaths is the most reliable bedside confirmation.
  • Monitoring ventilation during general anaesthesia, sedation, transport and in the ICU.
  • Early warning of disconnection, leaks, obstruction and rebreathing, usually before SpO₂ changes.
  • CPR. ETCO₂ reflects the quality of compressions, and a sudden rise often signals return of spontaneous circulation.
  • Spotting emergencies such as pulmonary embolism, air embolism and malignant hyperthermia.

§ 08

The Physics: Four Things Light Can Do

Reflection, transmission, absorption, scattering

When light meets matter, the incident energy is split four ways:

Conservation of light
Incident = reflected + transmitted + absorbed + scattered
  • Reflection: the light bounces off a surface, with angle in equal to angle out.
  • Transmission: the light passes straight through.
  • Absorption: the photon energy matches a molecular transition and is taken up, ending as heat.
  • Scattering: the light is redirected in many directions by molecules or particles.

Infrared capnometry relies on absorption. Raman spectroscopy relies on scattering. Everything else follows from that difference.

§ 09

Infrared Absorption: How Most Capnometers Work

CO₂ absorbs at about 4.26 µm

CO₂ absorbs infrared radiation strongly at about 4.26 µm. A capnometer passes infrared light through the gas sample, and a detector measures how much arrives at the other side. The more CO₂ present, the less light gets through. The Beer-Lambert law links the two, so the monitor can convert transmitted light into a CO₂ concentration or partial pressure.

The common design is non-dispersive infrared (NDIR). A broadband source and an optical filter select the CO₂ wavelength band, so no prism or grating is needed. That keeps the device small, cheap and robust.

Two practical points that come up in exams

Nitrous oxide overlaps. N₂O absorbs near 4.5 µm, close to the CO₂ band, and can broaden the absorption. Monitors correct for this.

Homonuclear molecules are invisible. O₂ and N₂ have no change in dipole moment when they vibrate, so they do not absorb infrared. IR cannot measure them.

§ 10

Raman Scattering: The Fingerprint Approach

Inelastic scattering, one laser, every gas

Fig 2 — When a photon meets a molecule

When monochromatic laser light hits a gas molecule, most of it is scattered with no change in energy. This is Rayleigh scattering, and it is elastic. A very small fraction is scattered with a shifted energy. This is Raman scattering, and it is inelastic.

  • Stokes: the molecule takes up some energy, so the scattered photon has a longer wavelength.
  • Anti-Stokes: the molecule gives up energy, so the scattered photon has a shorter wavelength.

Fig 3 — Energy levels: Rayleigh, Stokes and anti-Stokes

The size of the shift depends on the molecule’s vibrational energy levels, so each gas produces its own set of peaks. A single laser and one detector can therefore identify and quantify O₂, N₂, CO₂, N₂O and the volatile agents together, including the gases IR cannot see. Stokes lines are usually stronger at room temperature, because most molecules start in the lowest vibrational level.

Raman gas analysers were used clinically, for example in multi-gas systems such as the Ohmeda RASCAL.

§ 11

Why Raman Did Not Win

The problem is signal strength

Raman scattering is extremely weak, roughly a millionth of the Rayleigh signal or less. Detecting it needs a laser, precise optics and a sensitive detector. That made the monitors bulkier, more costly and less rugged than IR units.

§ 12

So Which Is Ideal?

It depends on how the question is asked

  • Most feasible and widely used for capnometry: infrared absorption. It is accurate, fast enough for breath-by-breath monitoring, and cheap and compact enough to fit every theatre and ICU.
  • Analytically the most versatile: Raman scattering. If cost and size did not matter, one instrument could measure every respiratory and anaesthetic gas at once.
Viva tip

State both and then commit: IR absorption wins in practice because the CO₂ signal is strong and specific, and a cheap, small, reliable sensor matters more than the ability to measure every gas.

Other methods worth a mention:

  • Mass spectrometry: accurate and multi-gas, but bulky, so it was mostly used as a shared central system.
  • Colorimetric detectors: a disposable pH-sensitive indicator that changes colour with CO₂, used to confirm tracheal intubation.

§ 14

A Short Relay

1928 to the 1990s

  • 1928: C. V. Raman discovers the inelastic scattering of light, and wins the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1930.
  • 1943: Karl Luft develops the non-dispersive infrared gas analyser that capnometers still build on.
  • Early 1990s: capnography becomes an accepted standard of monitoring during general anaesthesia.
  • Always: George Stokes, whom you met in the pulse oximeter story, gave his name to the Stokes shift behind Raman scattering.
§ 15

Key Points

For your next viva

  1. The pulse oximeter measures oxygenation. Capnography measures ventilation, perfusion and metabolism.
  2. Capnometry gives the number. Capnography gives the waveform, and the waveform holds most of the information.
  3. IR capnometry uses absorption of IR light at about 4.26 µm.
  4. Raman uses inelastic scattering and gives a fingerprint for each gas, including O₂ and N₂.
  5. Raman’s weak signal made it costly and bulky, so IR remains the standard.
  6. ETCO₂ is not PaCO₂, and a flat trace needs action, not assumptions.
